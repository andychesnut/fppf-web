## 1. The bottom line

1. **The free Snapshot can launch.** Nothing found blocks it, provided the eleven product rules in section 2 hold.
2. **The paid Full Assessment is the real legal question.** A report built from one person's answers, sold for a fee, that touches investing, sits close to the line that defines an "investment adviser". This is the one place the GM cannot settle the answer from public sources.
3. **A recurring program adds a second body of law** (automatic renewal rules). A one-time or fixed-term product avoids it entirely. Launch paid as one-time only.
4. **Paid referrals to advisers stay off.** In several states the person paid for the referral must register. Keep hypothesis 4 (expert routing) behind the gate.
5. **Trademark: one new lead needs checking.** A 2023 application for "FIRST PRINCIPLES FINANCIAL" in financial advisory services exists. Its current status is unverified.
6. **Recommendation on outside counsel: go, limited scope, before paid checkout turns on.** Not needed for the free Snapshot. Three questions, listed in section 11. Cost is a new spend outside the envelope, so it is the Principal's decision.

## 3. The investment adviser and "advice" line

**The law.** Federal law defines an investment adviser as a person who, for compensation, engages in the business of advising others, directly or through publications or writings, on the value of securities or the advisability of investing in, buying, or selling them. (15 U.S.C. 80b-2(a)(11). Read.)

**The three elements, as SEC staff describe them** (staff memo, Read; the memo warns it may not be current):
- **Compensation.** "The receipt of any economic benefit suffices." It need not be labeled an advisory fee.
- **In the business.** Met if the person holds itself out as giving investment advice, gets separate pay for it, or typically advises on specific securities or categories of securities.
- **Advice about securities.** Broad. Staff say it can include advice about market trends, advice on the advantages of investing in securities instead of other investments, and a list of securities to choose from even with no specific recommendation.

All three must be met. Staff construe each broadly.

**The exclusion that matters to us.** The definition excludes "the publisher of any bona fide newspaper, news magazine or business or financial publication of general and regular circulation" (clause D. Read). In Lowe v. SEC, 472 U.S. 181 (1985), the Supreme Court held that disinterested newsletters offered to the public on a regular schedule fall inside that exclusion, and said Congress was "primarily interested in regulating the business of rendering personalized investment advice" (Read, opinion text). SEC staff state the test as three parts: impersonal advice not tailored to a specific client, bona fide (disinterested, not promotional), and general and regular circulation.

**State law.** Small advisers are regulated by the states, not the SEC. State definitions and exclusions vary. Not surveyed this run. Unverified.

**Applied to FPPF.**

| Product | GM read | Why |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Free Snapshot | Low risk with R1 to R4 | It scores habits and resilience from bands and returns templated education. Four of 24 questions touch investing (employer match, investing while carrying high-rate debt, tax-advantaged accounts, share in speculation). The risk is in the result text for the Features stage, not in asking. |
| Site content and email | Low risk | General, impersonal, same for everyone. Closest to the publisher exclusion. |
| Paid Full Assessment and plan | **Open question for counsel** | It is for compensation, and it is built from one person's answers. That weakens the "impersonal" leg of the exclusion. Whether it is "advice about securities" then depends on what the Features and Freedom sections say. If they stay on behavior and sequence (cash first, high-rate debt before investing, capture a match) the argument that no securities advice is given is reasonable but not certain. Staff's "advantages of investing in securities instead of other investments" language is the exposure. |
| Stage program (recurring) | Same question as above | Same content risk, plus section 9. |
| Expert routing for a fee | **High risk. Stays off.** | See section 10. |

**GM position.** Build the Full Assessment so the question barely arises: rules-based and templated, no securities content, no allocation, no market views, with the investing sections limited to general education and pointing people to licensed professionals for anything specific. Then have counsel confirm before checkout goes live. A wrong guess here is unregistered advisory activity, which is not a risk to take on the GM's reading.

## 4. A related trap: the Safeguards Rule

**The law.** The FTC Safeguards Rule (16 CFR Part 314, eCFR current to 2026-08-13, Read) covers "financial institutions", defined by activity, not label. Listed examples include "an investment advisory company and a credit counseling service" and a "finder" that brings buyers and sellers together. A person is a "customer" if they obtain "financial, investment, or economic advisory services from you for a fee". Businesses not "significantly engaged" in financial activities are out.

**Applied.** A free educational quiz is probably outside this. The pages do not address it, so that is the GM's inference, not a finding. A paid assessment framed as financial advisory services could bring it in. If covered, a written security program is required. Businesses holding customer information on fewer than 5,000 consumers are excused from four parts (written risk assessment, penetration testing and monitoring, written incident response plan, annual board report) but not the rest.

**GM position.** Keep collecting bands and not balances, which is already the design. Add this as question 2 for counsel. The describe-ourselves-as-education rule (R4) helps here too.

## 5. FTC: claims, endorsements, reviews

**The law.**
- Section 5 of the FTC Act bars deceptive and unfair practices. Claims need support before they are made.
- The Endorsement Guides (16 CFR Part 255) are guidance without the force of law, but practices that conflict with them can draw a Section 5 case. Material connections (payment, free product, affiliate pay) must be disclosed clearly. If a testimonial shows results people will not generally get, the ad must say what is generally expected. "Results not typical" does not fix it. The company is responsible for what its endorsers say. (FTC FAQ, Read, partly from an extracted summary.)
- The Consumer Reviews and Testimonials Rule (16 CFR Part 465) took effect 2024-10-21 and allows civil penalties for knowing violations. It bars fake reviews and testimonials, buying reviews conditioned on sentiment, undisclosed insider reviews, company-controlled "independent" review sites, review suppression by threat, and buying fake social proof. Incentives for honest reviews are allowed if no sentiment is required. (FTC Q&A, Read.)

**Applied.**
- **Outcome claims.** The floor already bans guarantees. Add: no "users improved by X" until re-assessment data exists and supports it.
- **Months of No.** A computed estimate is fine when the math is shown and the inputs are the person's own. Do not present it as a prediction.
- **Price-intent test.** Showing a price for something not yet sold is acceptable if it is unmistakably labeled as not available, with no payment taken (R7). Showing different prices to different visitors is a test, not a promise. When the product ships, honor the lowest price shown to anyone who clicked "notify me", or say clearly that the launch price may differ. The GM chooses: say on the button that the launch price may differ.
- **Reviews.** When we ask for them, ask every user, publish negative ones on the same schedule, never pay for sentiment.

## 6. Email: CAN-SPAM

**The law** (FTC compliance guide, Read):
1. Accurate header information.
2. No deceptive subject lines.
3. Identify the message as an ad.
4. Include a valid physical postal address. A registered PO box or a registered private mailbox qualifies.
5. Tell people how to opt out, by reply email or a single web page.
6. Honor opt-outs within 10 business days. The opt-out must work for at least 30 days after sending. No fee, and no information required beyond the email address.
7. You remain responsible for what vendors send for you.

The guide states a maximum of $53,088 per violating email. That figure was edited in January 2024. Whether it has been adjusted since is unverified.

**Opt-in does not exempt us.** The law covers all commercial email. People who signed up can still opt out.

**Transactional versus commercial.** The result email a person asks for is likely transactional (delivering what was agreed). The 90-day re-check and any update email are commercial if they promote the product. The categories are read narrowly.

**GM position.** Treat every email as commercial (R8). It costs nothing and removes the classification question. The existing rule that the app sends nothing while the postal address is empty is correct and stays.

**Outreach.** The floor already bars unsolicited outreach. This memo does not lift it. Cold email is legal under CAN-SPAM if the seven rules are met, but platform and vendor rules (Postmark's terms were not read this run, unverified) are stricter, and it is a poor first impression for a trust product. Stays off.

## 7. Privacy and state data laws

**California privacy policy law (CalOPPA).** No size threshold. Any operator of a commercial website that collects personally identifiable information from California residents must post a privacy policy conspicuously. (Bus. & Prof. Code 22575, Read on Justia.) The policy must state:
1. Categories of information collected and categories of third parties it may be shared with.
2. The process to review and request changes to one's information, if one exists.
3. How users are told of material changes.
4. The effective date.
5. How the site responds to Do Not Track signals.
6. Whether other parties collect information about a user's activity across sites.

A violation arises only if the policy is not posted within 30 days of being notified.

**CCPA.** Applies to for-profit businesses that meet any one of: gross annual revenue over a threshold, personal information of 100,000 or more consumers or households bought, sold, or shared, or 50% or more of revenue from selling or sharing personal information. The two official pages disagree on the revenue figure: the Attorney General's page says $25 million, the privacy agency's page says $26,625,000. Either way FPPF is far below all three. **Not applicable now.** Revisit at 50,000 California records.

**Other states.** 20 states have comprehensive privacy laws in force and 4 more are scheduled (Secondary, third-party tracker). Most use consumer-count thresholds we do not meet. Two have no numeric threshold:
- **Texas.** Small businesses as defined by the SBA are generally exempt, but must get consent before selling sensitive data. (Secondary.)
- **Nebraska.** No numeric threshold per the tracker. Whether it has the same small-business exemption is unverified.

We never sell data, so the Texas duty is met by design. Thresholds for the other 16 states were not read. Unverified.

**Applied.** The design already fits: bands not balances, no SSN, no account numbers, email only if given, delete link in every email, 18 or older. Add:
- The privacy policy lists every vendor category that touches data (hosting, database, email).
- The first-party `events` table means no third-party trackers. State that in the policy. Do not add an ad pixel without revisiting this section.
- Honor a delete request for everyone, not only residents of states that require it.
- If a breach ever happens, state breach-notice laws apply in every state regardless of size. Not surveyed. Unverified.

## 8. Terms of service

**The law.** Online terms bind a user who lacks actual knowledge only if (1) the site gives reasonably conspicuous notice of the terms and (2) the user takes an action, such as clicking a button or checking a box, that unambiguously shows assent. In Berman v. Freedom Financial Network (9th Cir. 2022), terms failed because the notice was in tiny gray text and the button did not say that clicking meant agreeing. (Secondary: case page and a law-firm summary. The opinion itself was not read.)

**Applied (R10).**
- Put the agreement sentence directly beside the button, in normal body size, with links that look like links.
- Name the action: "By saving your map you agree to...".
- Keep the terms short: educational purpose, no advice, no professional relationship, age 18 or older, accuracy depends on the user's answers, limitation of liability, governing law, how terms change.
- **No arbitration clause for now.** It adds drafting risk and is the clause courts scrutinize hardest. A question for counsel when paid launches.
- The terms name the legal entity. They cannot be final until `LEGAL_ENTITY_NAME` is filled in.

## 9. Refunds and recurring billing

**One-time purchase.** No federal rule found that requires a refund for a digital product. What is required is honesty: state the policy before payment and follow it. State rules on refund disclosure and the payment processor's own terms were not read. Unverified.

**GM position.** Offer a plain 14-day, no-questions refund on the Full Assessment. It is the customer-first choice and a low-cost trust signal. Pricing and refund terms are the GM's call under the standing ruling.

**Recurring billing. Three layers:**
- **Federal statute (ROSCA).** Online sellers of recurring plans must (1) disclose all material terms clearly before taking billing information, (2) get express informed consent before charging, and (3) give a simple way to stop the charges. (Secondary.)
- **FTC "click to cancel" rule.** Vacated in full by the Eighth Circuit on 2025-07-08. The FTC restarted rulemaking by sending an advance notice to the White House regulatory office on 2026-01-30. Status after February 2026 is unverified. ROSCA enforcement continues: the summary cites a $7.5 million Chegg settlement and a pending case against Uber. (Secondary.)
- **California Automatic Renewal Law, as amended from 2025-07-01.** Separate express consent to the renewal itself, amount and frequency shown before billing details are confirmed, consent records kept for three years or one year after termination, a prominent "click to cancel" path, notice of a price change 7 to 30 days ahead, and an annual reminder. (Secondary: a law-firm summary written before the effective date. The statute text was not read.) Other states have their own versions. Not surveyed.

**GM position (R11).** Sell the stage program first as a fixed 90-day term for one payment, with no renewal. That keeps hypothesis 2 testable and stays outside every rule in this section. Move to auto-renewal only after the California list above is built into checkout and account pages.

## 10. Referrals and solicitation

**The law.**
- **The adviser's side.** SEC Rule 206(4)-1 lets an adviser pay "promoters" for testimonials, endorsements, and referrals, with disclosure of the pay and conflicts, a written agreement above $1,000 in 12 months, and a bar on disqualified persons. (SEC guide, Read.)
- **The promoter's side.** The rule does not say when a paid promoter must itself register. A promoter "may, depending on the facts and circumstances, be acting as an investment adviser". SEC staff list "advice about the selection of an investment adviser" as something that can satisfy the advice element. It is usually a state question. California is described as treating a paid solicitor as meeting the adviser definition and needing registration. New York subjects solicitors to registration and exams. (Secondary: Kitces. State statutes not read.)
- **FTC.** Any paid referral or affiliate link is a material connection that must be disclosed (section 5).
- **Safeguards Rule.** A "finder" is a listed example of a financial institution (section 4).

**Applied.** Paid routing to advisers would most likely require registration in at least some states, would bring the Safeguards Rule in, and creates the independence conflict already noted in the run log. **It stays behind the gate. The GM recommends ranking it last or dropping it.** Unpaid, general pointers ("for a specific plan, a fee-only fiduciary adviser is one option") are education and are fine.

Affiliate links to non-securities products (a budgeting tool, a book) are legally simpler: disclose clearly next to the link. Still not for launch. Independence is the product.

## 12. Outside counsel: recommendation

**Go, limited scope, timed before paid checkout. Not needed to launch the free Snapshot.**

**Why go.** Sections 3 and 4 turn on judgment about facts (how personalized the report is, what the investing sections say) and on state law the GM did not and cannot reliably survey. The downside of being wrong is operating as an unregistered adviser. The other areas are checklists the GM can meet without help.

**Scope: three questions, one fixed-fee engagement.**
1. Does the Full Assessment as designed (attach the actual report template) make the operator an investment adviser under federal law or the law of the operator's home state? What must change if so?
2. Does it make the operator a financial institution under the Safeguards Rule?
3. Trademark clearance on the chosen brand, plus a one-hour review of the terms and privacy policy.

**What to send counsel:** this memo, the report template, the result-page copy, the terms and privacy drafts.

**Cost.** The GM has no verified quote. GM estimate, unmeasured: $1,500 to $4,000 for a fixed-fee limited review by a securities or fintech solo practitioner, more at a large firm. This is above the $100 monthly envelope, so it is a new spend and the Principal's decision.

**If the answer is no go on counsel:** the fallback is to ship the Full Assessment with zero investing content (stop at Fundamentals, treat Features and Freedom as general articles that are the same for everyone). That lowers the risk substantially without removing it.
